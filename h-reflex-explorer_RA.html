The whole picture

One stimulus, two routes, one collision.

The M-wave is the muscle's direct response to motor axons fired at the electrode. The H-reflex is a monosynaptic spinal reflex started by Ia afferents fired at the same electrode. As current rises, the motor axons also carry spikes back towards the cord, and these cancel the returning reflex.

The sequence to explain (FCR, 2–10 mA)

  1. 2–3 mA: the largest axons, the Ia afferents, fire first. A small H-reflex appears with no M-wave.
  2. 3–5 mA: more Ia input recruits more motor neurons, smallest first (size principle). H approaches Hmax.
  3. ≈ 4 mA: motor axons start firing directly. The earlier M-wave appears.
  4. 5–8 mA: M grows. Antidromic motor spikes block more and more reflex spikes, so H falls.
  5. 9–10 mA: M reaches Mmax. Every motor axon is blocked and H disappears.

Three analogies

The direct highway (M): the impulse runs straight from the electrode to the muscle.

The spinal detour (H): the impulse runs up the Ia afferent, crosses one synapse, and comes back down the motor axon. Same destination, longer trip.

Two trains, one track (collision): spikes travelling in opposite directions in the same axon meet head-on. Each leaves refractory membrane behind it, so neither can pass.

Spikes spread both ways from the electrode

The current only flows locally, under the electrode. The patch of axon it depolarises has resting membrane on both sides, so the action potential spreads in both directions.

In Ia afferents, the spike heading to the spindle goes nowhere: there is no synapse there to pass it on. In motor axons, the spike heading to the cord is the one that collides with the reflex.

The M-wave misconception

The recorded M-wave never visits the spinal cord. It comes from impulses travelling orthodromically from the electrode to the muscle.

The same stimulus also sends antidromic impulses up those motor axons. They are not the M-wave, but they cause the collision.

About this teaching model

The FCR preset mirrors the class practical: median-nerve stimulation at the elbow, flexor carpi radialis surface EMG, 1 ms constant-current pulses, and a 2–10 mA sweep in 1 mA steps. Its latencies are calibrated to the manual's expected windows (M 10–15 ms, H 18–25 ms), so the delay from motor end-plates to the recording electrodes is set longer than in most published onset values. Soleus is a comparison preparation with typical published latencies.

How it works. A pool of 150 motor neurons is recruited by the Ia volley in order of size (Henneman's size principle). The electrode fires the largest axons first: Ia afferents before motor axons. Any motor axon fired directly carries an antidromic spike that cancels its reflex spike. Pulses move with correct relative timing; distances in the drawing are not to scale. H amplitude varies about 5% from trial to trial; M about 1%.

Keyboard: Space = Stimulate · ← → = current ± 1 mA · P = pause.

In the real practical, leave at least 5–10 s between stimuli to avoid post-activation depression.
